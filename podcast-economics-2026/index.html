What podcasts actually earn in 2026

Five minutes on the real numbers behind the weekly question. None of this is an argument against weekly. It's the ground we'd be deciding on.

Compiled July 2026 from platform data, verified against an independent second review

27
Downloads the median podcast episode gets in its first week.

Not the median new show. The median episode, across roughly 113,000 shows on one of the largest hosting platforms. Most podcasts are much smaller than the ones we listen to.

Where the numbers sit

A show doing one to three thousand downloads an episode is already in the top few percent. That's worth knowing before we set expectations for ourselves.

Ads won't pay for this. Not in year one.

$15–30

What advertisers pay per thousand listens for a host-read spot. The higher numbers you hear quoted for niche shows didn't hold up when we checked them.

~5,000

Downloads an episode needs before ad marketplaces will sell your inventory at all. Below that, nobody's buying.

$25–125

What a single ad spot is actually worth at one to five thousand downloads. Real, and not close to a production budget.

So we should plan on advertising revenue being zero for season one. Sponsors we go out and sell ourselves are a different thing, roughly $100 to $300 an episode, but that's a sales job, not income that shows up.

Breaking even is rare, and nobody can tell you when

85% earn nothing at all

Of independent podcasters surveyed, most never make a dollar. At most about one in ten ever reaches break-even.

There is no "typical" timeline

We went looking for a credible number on how long break-even takes and there isn't one. Anything you've read saying twelve to eighteen months is unsourced. Costs repeat every season, so time by itself doesn't get you there.

Which means the show isn't the business

For two people who already have an audience and things to offer, the show is how people find us. It isn't a thing that pays for itself, and it doesn't need to be. That's a real model, not a consolation prize, but it changes what we're optimizing for.

Ranked by what actually returns, best first:

1

People who find us and then work with us

By a wide margin the strongest economics, especially anything one-to-one. A handful of people is meaningful here, which is not true of any other lane.

2

Paid subscribers

Real, but there's no trustworthy benchmark for how many free readers become paying ones. Plan on a small percentage and treat season one as the experiment that tells us.

3

Sponsors we approach directly

Possible at our size, better when bundled with the newsletter, and it only happens if someone is doing the asking.

4

Live tapings

Great for the community around the show. Thin as a margin business on its own.

✕

Ad marketplaces

Off the table until we're several times bigger. Worth revisiting, not worth planning around.

Two things this changes for us

We have to be able to see it working

If the show's value is people finding us, we need to know when that happens. That means tagged links and one question at intake asking how someone found us, in place from the first episode. Otherwise we'll be guessing at the end of the season.

We pay together, we benefit separately

We'd be splitting the costs down the middle while the returns land in each of our own work. That's fine, it just means agreeing now on how we'll judge whether it's worth continuing, rather than comparing notes later and finding we measured different things.

One more that's easy to miss: our two audiences aren't simply added together. There's overlap, and plenty of people on any list have gone quiet. Getting people to move to a brand new place to read is the hard part, so season one is hundreds of people, not thousands.

What I take from it

Weekly is better for building a habit and I'm not arguing with that. But since none of this pays for itself in season one, the only question that matters is what we're willing to spend to find out whether the show does its actual job, which is bringing people to us. That's a budget conversation, not a cadence one.
